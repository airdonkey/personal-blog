Many parents are raising children with a map that once worked remarkably well.

Study hard. Accept delayed gratification. Enter a better university. Find secure and respectable work. For people from ordinary families, this sequence offered more than academic success. It offered a way to turn time, discipline and examination results into movement through society.

The map was never equally available to everyone, and it never guaranteed an easy life. But it was legible. A young person could usually see the next gate and understand, at least in broad terms, what would open it.

Now many of the people who followed that map have become parents. We still believe in education, yet we are preparing children for a world in which education no longer provides one clear route upward. That is our second examination. This time there is no published syllabus, no stable marking schedule and no single answer sheet.

## Education changed my family before it changed me

Stories of educational mobility are often told as first-generation stories. Mine is slightly different. My mother was among the early university students after China's national university entrance examination was restored in the late 1970s. By the time I went to school, my family already trusted education and understood how to organise a child's life around it.

I did well in the national entrance examination and went to university in Beijing. In that limited sense, I was a second-generation beneficiary of the examination system: not the first person in the family to discover the route, but the next person to travel farther along it.

That distinction matters. I did not inherit wealth, powerful connections or an insider's knowledge of elite institutions. What I inherited was a belief: reading, disciplined work and academic achievement could change the range of choices available to a family. That belief was neither naïve nor false. I am living evidence that it sometimes worked.

I am now a parent, a migrant and a secondary-school teacher in New Zealand. These positions let me see the same question from different sides. What follows is not a statistical comparison of China and New Zealand, and it is not a universal theory of parenting. It is an essay grounded in family history, migration and classroom experience. Where I move beyond those experiences, I am offering a working hypothesis rather than a proven result.

## What the first examination taught us

China's national university entrance examination is commonly known as the *gaokao*. Its rules and papers vary across places and have changed over time, but its social meaning is widely understood: performance in a small number of high-stakes examinations plays a central role in access to universities.

For ordinary families, the attraction of such a system is not that it makes society perfectly fair. It does not. The attraction is that it makes one important route unusually visible. Money, location and family knowledge still shape preparation, but examination marks can be read by people outside one's family and hometown. They are portable evidence of achievement.

The system also teaches habits beyond the subjects being examined. It teaches students to convert a distant ambition into daily work; to tolerate repetition; to keep going when immediate rewards are absent; and to believe that a difficult problem probably has a correct method hidden inside it.

These habits travelled with many of us. They helped us leave smaller places, survive demanding universities and enter large professional organisations. They became not only a study method but also a moral language. To work hard was to be serious. To endure was to be responsible. To plan ahead was to care for one's family.

The danger is that a method which once produced real mobility can begin to look like a law of life.

## The route did not disappear; it multiplied

Education still matters. Credentials still matter. Strong reading, mathematics and subject knowledge still create opportunities. The mistake is not believing in education; it is assuming that education now converts into opportunity through the same narrow sequence.

The world facing today's children contains more routes and more hidden conditions. Academic results interact with subject choices, communication, confidence, wellbeing, information, social networks, institutional knowledge and the ability to move between different systems. A family may need to decide not only how much a child should study, but which curriculum, which pathway, which country and which definition of success it is preparing for.

Migration made this change especially visible to me. In New Zealand, I encountered a school culture, assessment structure and pace of learning different from the ones that had formed me in China. Some differences create room for exploration; others leave parents uncertain about whether enough is being learned. Some students benefit from flexibility; others need more structure than they receive. The contrast cannot honestly be reduced to one country being rigorous and the other relaxed, or one producing knowledge while the other produces creativity.

Different systems distribute clarity, pressure and responsibility differently. Moving between them forces families to make judgements that no examination result can make on their behalf.

## Why successful students can become anxious parents

Here is my working hypothesis: parents who learned to succeed under clear academic rules may be unusually vulnerable when the rules become ambiguous.

Our competence was built by finding the standard, practising toward it and reducing mistakes. When our children encounter uncertainty, we instinctively apply the same sequence. We search for more information, add another activity, compare another school or try to identify the missing skill. The more conscientious the parent, the longer the invisible checklist can become.

This response is understandable. Parents from ordinary backgrounds know that missed opportunities can be expensive. We may not possess enough wealth to treat education as an experiment. Preparation feels like care, and control can feel like protection.

But ambiguity cannot always be defeated by adding tasks. A child may not need the next course; the child may need time to discover what is worth sustained effort. A disappointing result may not reveal a knowledge gap; it may reveal exhaustion, fear, weak motivation or a mismatch between the goal and the person pursuing it. A parent's successful strategy can become noise if it answers a question the child is not actually facing.

The first examination rewarded us for solving the problem in front of us. The second asks whether we have identified the right problem at all.

## What cannot be passed down unchanged

We cannot simply pass down the timing of our own success. Learning more, earlier, may produce an advantage in one system and unnecessary pressure in another. A sequence that was efficient twenty or thirty years ago may prepare a child for a competition that has already changed.

We cannot pass down one secure image of a respectable career. Industries rise and decline; technologies redistribute the value of established skills; migration can reprice a professional identity almost overnight. My own movement from financial work in China to education in New Zealand taught me how quickly competence can lose its recognised value when the surrounding system changes.

Most importantly, we cannot pass down the assumption that effort and outcome form a clean equation. Effort remains necessary in many fields, but its returns are shaped by resources, timing, health, institutions and chance. Children need the strength to work; they also need the judgement to notice when a strategy is no longer working.

## What remains worth passing on

The failure of an old formula does not make its ingredients worthless.

Reading deeply still enlarges the world a person can enter. Practising a difficult skill still changes what the mind and body can do. Keeping promises, finishing necessary work and accepting responsibility still make someone dependable. The ability to continue without immediate applause remains a form of freedom.

For families without large stores of inherited advantage, it would be careless to romanticise spontaneity and tell children that effort no longer matters. The language of effortless self-discovery can itself be a luxury when other families are quietly investing knowledge, time and money.

What must change is the object of effort. Instead of preparing children to eliminate every uncertainty, we can help them develop the capacity to act under uncertainty. Instead of optimising every year of childhood for admission, we can preserve the curiosity, health and relationships that make long learning possible. Instead of giving them our finished answer, we can show them how an answer is tested, revised and sometimes abandoned.

## The subjects in the second examination

The second examination tests parental judgement more than parental knowledge.

It asks whether we can separate a child's difficulty from our own fear. It asks whether we know what is worth pursuing and what can be left undone. It asks whether we can allocate finite family resources without converting every decision into a referendum on the child's future.

It also tests whether we recognise the child as a person rather than a mobility project. The purpose of education cannot be merely to reproduce the parent's status, recover the parent's lost opportunities or protect the family from every downward movement. A young person eventually needs the authority to form goals that are not exact copies of ours.

This does not require parents to withdraw. Children still need guidance, boundaries and access to knowledge they cannot yet find alone. The harder discipline is to offer structure without occupying the entire space of decision—to remain responsible while allowing another person gradually to become responsible too.

## A different definition of upward movement

My generation often understood mobility spatially and institutionally: leave the small town, enter the large city, join the better university, reach the stronger organisation. Those movements changed real lives, including mine.

The next generation may still make some of the same moves. But in a less predictable world, upward movement must also mean increasing the ability to choose again. A person with knowledge but no agency can be trapped by the first system that rewards them. A person with confidence but no discipline may never turn possibility into competence. Education worthy of the name should help hold both together.

Our children will still sit examinations. The deeper question is whether, after the marks are issued, they can recognise the forces shaping their choices, learn what a new situation demands and construct a life that is not merely an answer to someone else's paper.

The first examination asked many of us to change our own lives through education. The second asks us to help the next generation build lives without insisting that they repeat ours. There is no model answer. Perhaps passing begins when we stop trying to sit the examination in their place.
